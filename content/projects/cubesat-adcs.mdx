## Overview

A full closed-loop ADCS/flight-software stack for a single satellite, built
on top of [Spacecraft Dynamics Simulation](/projects/spacecraft-dynamics-simulation):
multiplicative-EKF attitude estimation (star tracker primary, sun+magnetometer
TRIAD fallback), PID/LQR/cascaded attitude control auto-tuned from bus
inertia, a 4-wheel reaction-wheel pyramid plus magnetorquer cluster for
detumble and momentum desaturation, an autonomous fault-detection/mode-manager
layer, and a real orbit (two-body + J2, Sun/Moon third-body perturbation,
atmospheric drag, solar radiation pressure, eclipse-gated power) — all flown
against the same plain-data flight-software interface a real flight computer
would run. The mission concept is a Reflect-Orbital-style spacecraft: an 18m
deployable mirror that bounces sunlight onto a ground target, on top of the
usual Nadir/Sun/Detumble/Target/Slew/Fine-pointing modes.

## Problem

I wanted to go past "physics engine with a controller bolted on" and build
something closer to how flight software actually gets validated: a stack
that never reads ground truth, runs on a fixed cyclic rate, and has to keep
flying through the failures a real mission runs into. That meant:

- Attitude estimation good enough to fly on, not just "read `body->orientation`" —
  including the routine dropouts (a blinded/slewing-too-fast star tracker) a
  real system estimates through
- Fault handling that's a first-class part of the architecture, not a
  bolt-on check, since real spacecraft lose wheels and battery margin and
  still have to keep pointing at *something* defensible
- Real orbital dynamics — J2 drift, eclipse, third-body perturbation — driving
  the sensor/power models instead of a kinematic stand-in, since a lot of
  what makes ADCS interesting (eclipse-gated power, ground-station geometry,
  sun-relative fallback pointing) only shows up once the orbit is real
- A hardware-abstraction boundary strict enough that the same flight software
  could plausibly run against a HIL rig or real hardware without changes

## Technical Architecture

- **`ADCS`/`FDIR`.** Fully hardware-abstracted — no `RigidBody`, no
  `PhysicsWorld`, nothing but plain per-sensor readings in and commanded
  actuator values out. `ADCS` owns a multiplicative EKF (propagate on gyro,
  correct on star tracker or a TRIAD fallback), guidance for seven pointing
  modes, three swappable attitude controllers, B-dot detumble, and
  cross-product-law momentum desaturation. `FDIR` sits beside it as a peer,
  not nested inside it — the same "mode manager as its own module" pattern
  NASA cFS/JPL F' use — reading `ADCS`'s freshly-updated telemetry each
  cycle and resolving a commanded-vs-actually-flown mode split (a wheel-loss
  or low-battery fault forces `SUN_POINTING`; excess rate forces `DETUMBLE`
  outright) without `ADCS` ever running its own fault checks.
- **`FlightSoftware`.** The one place simulated hardware is actually
  touched — deliberately not a `Satellite` method. Each sensor is sampled
  independently at its own realistic firmware rate rather than one bundled
  per-cycle snapshot: IMU/magnetometer/wheel telemetry every cycle (real
  hardware for all three comfortably exceeds a 20 Hz loop), star tracker at
  5 Hz, sun sensor at 10 Hz, battery telemetry at 1 Hz, matching real
  fuel-gauge/star-tracker/sensor-bus polling rates. A cycle without a fresh
  star-tracker frame reports "no new reading" rather than resending the last
  one, so the EKF never re-corrects against a now-stale attitude.
- **Orbit.** A real single-satellite orbit propagated by the engine's
  `rigidbody/orbit/` module — two-body + J2 for the spacecraft, a
  `CelestialSystem` Sun→Earth→Moon hierarchy for third-body perturbation and
  eclipse geometry, atmospheric drag and solar-radiation pressure on the
  predicted ground track. Ground-station targeting and EPS generation both
  read off this real state, not a placeholder.

## Implementation Details

The FSW code (`ADCS`/`FDIR`/`FlightSoftware`) follows real embedded-firmware
constraints on purpose: fixed-size arrays sized by compile-time constants, no
dynamic allocation and no RNG anywhere in the hot path, enforced by a grep
check that's part of every change. Guidance, control, and actuator
allocation are all in body frame with an explicit "same sign convention
`allocateActuators()`'s Moore-Penrose pseudoinverse assumes" discipline,
since a mismatched sign here doesn't crash, it just silently destabilizes —
B-dot and desaturation both went through a headless-reproduction pass to
pin down the correct sign against the engine's actual reaction-torque
convention before either law was trustworthy.

Configuration is split by concern rather than one flat file:
`SatelliteConfig` (this spacecraft's physical design and onboard FSW task
rates), `VisualizationConfig` (camera/rendering/UI, never affects simulated
behavior), and `PhysicalConstants` (GM/solar-flux values that don't depend
on which satellite is flying). `HardwareConfig` — the plain-data mirror of
wheel/torquer geometry `ADCS::configure()` needs — is built from the exact
same loop that constructs the real simulated actuators, so it can't quietly
drift out of sync with what's actually flying.

## Challenges

The most misleading bug wasn't in the flight software at all, it was in the
renderer, and it looked exactly like a scale-factor mistake. Reaction wheels
and magnetorquers were drawing at the satellite's center instead of their
real mount offsets once the satellite actually sat at a real orbital
position (~6.9e6 m from the origin). My first assumption was a missing
`SATELLITE_VISUAL_SCALE` multiply somewhere in the wheel-drawing code.

It wasn't. The actual cause was float32 precision: the engine computes
`sat->position + sat->orientation * mountPositionBody` internally, in
float32, before the renderer ever sees the result. At a ~6.9e6 m position,
float32's ULP there is around 0.8 m — larger than the ~0.03 m mount offset
itself — so that addition rounded the offset away almost entirely *before*
`SATELLITE_VISUAL_SCALE` ever got applied to it. No amount of scaling after
the fact could recover an offset that had already been rounded to noise.
The fix was reading `mountPositionBody` directly and applying
`SATELLITE_VISUAL_SCALE` to the *offset* first — turning a ~0.03 m value
into a ~600 m one, comfortably above that same ULP — before rotating and
adding it to the real position. Same category of bug as the constraint
solver's absolute-determinant threshold on the engine side: correct math,
wrong assumption about what scale it would actually run at.

## Results

The closed loop runs end to end in real time with a live GUI: FDIR
correctly forces `SUN_POINTING` on a simulated wheel-authority loss and
recovers on `clearLatchedFaults()`, the EKF holds a converged estimate
through periodic star-tracker dropouts via the TRIAD fallback, B-dot damps
a random deployment tumble against a real (weak) LEO field, and `REFLECT`
mode actually bisects the sun/target directions to bounce light onto a
ground target as the real orbit and Earth's rotation move both underneath
it. The persistent test suite (one executable per feature area, `ctest`-
driven) covers the EKF, detumble, FDIR, and EPS behavior against the plain
per-sensor interface, independent of the simulation harness.

## Lessons Learned

Hardware abstraction is worth defending even when it's inconvenient. It was
tempting more than once to let `ADCS` just read `Satellite` directly instead
of routing everything through plain sensor readings and commanded actuator
values — it would have been less code in the moment. Keeping that boundary
paid off directly: the whole EKF/FDIR/detumble test suite drives synthetic
sensor readings with no `rigidbody` dependency at all, and the one place
real hardware gets touched (`FlightSoftware`) stayed small and auditable
precisely because nothing else was allowed to reach around it.

## Future Work

- Hardware-in-the-loop bridge, reading real ADC/I2C sensor values into the
  same `FlightSoftware` entry point instead of a simulated `Satellite`
- Thermal modeling feeding into FDIR alongside the existing wheel/battery/
  attitude-uncertainty faults
- A real S-band downlink/link-budget model instead of geometric
  ground-station visibility alone
- Multi-fault scenarios (e.g. a wheel loss during a star-tracker dropout)
  to exercise FDIR's fault-priority ordering under compounding failures
